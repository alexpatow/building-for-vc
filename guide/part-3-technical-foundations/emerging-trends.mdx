## Overview

The useful question has changed from "where can we add an LLM?" to "how much of this workflow do we still need to build?" Model providers now supply more of the execution environment, integrations, and application experience. Meanwhile, decision models offer another way to put AI inside software without generating a written answer at every step.

For a small technical team at a fund, these changes affect where you spend your time: buying an application, connecting data to an existing assistant, building a narrow workflow, or maintaining a custom product.

## Claude and ChatGPT Are Becoming Application Platforms

The model providers are moving up the stack. They're supplying the place people do the work, not just the API underneath someone else's application.

[Claude for Financial Services](https://claude.com/solutions/financial-services) packages financial workflows and data integrations. Claude's Office integrations put assistance inside spreadsheets, documents, and presentations. That overlaps with work funds have traditionally bought specialist software for or asked their engineering team to build: research, financial modeling, and preparing investment materials.

[ChatGPT apps](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/12515353-building-with-the-apps-sdk) let developers bring tools and interfaces into ChatGPT. [Workspace agents](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001143/) add reusable workflows connected to apps, with shared and scheduled execution. [Claude's connectors](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11175166-get-started-with-custom-connectors-using-remote-mcp) similarly connect the assistant to external systems.

**What this changes for your fund**

Before building a research interface, try the workflow in the assistant your team already uses. A partner preparing for a founder meeting may need access to the CRM, past memos, and a briefing template. That may be enough to produce the briefing without a separate application.

Keep a dedicated interface where it earns its place: comparing a pipeline of companies, tracking approvals, reviewing exceptions, or entering structured data every week. A conversation is useful for an open-ended research question; a table can be better for reviewing fifty records.

Your investment shifts toward reliable data access, reusable instructions, and integration with the fund's records. Keep company IDs, source documents, and completed outputs available outside a single assistant's conversation history. If you change providers, you shouldn't have to reconstruct the portfolio from chat transcripts.

**People across the fund can build on the same foundation**

An investor may assemble a research workflow in an assistant while fund ops builds an interface in [Lovable](https://docs.lovable.dev/). You can support both with the same data and access controls. The [Common Mistakes chapter](/guide/part-1-understanding-vc/common-mistakes#you-dont-have-to-build-every-application-yourself) covers this division of work; [Security and Compliance](/guide/part-3-technical-foundations/security-and-compliance) covers the boundaries underneath it.

## MCP, APIs, and CLI Tools

[Model Context Protocol (MCP)](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/docs/getting-started/intro) is an open standard for connecting AI applications to tools and data. Use it when you want compatible assistants to discover and call your fund's tools. You may already have a vendor connector available, so check before writing your own server.

Expose operations that match the work: find a company, retrieve its interaction history, fetch a source document, or save a draft note. Those are easier to reuse across assistants than an integration that only serves one screen in your application.

For your own engineering workflow, an existing CLI or script may be enough. For a background integration, a regular API may be the simplest fit. These interfaces can wrap the same underlying service. You don't need to pick a winner between MCP and command-line tools.

Keep permissions and business rules in that service. Changing the client from a dashboard to an assistant shouldn't change what the caller can access.

## Decision APIs: Small Judgments Inside Software

[OpenAI's Decisions API](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions) evaluates evidence and returns typed answers: a probability that a condition is true, a choice from supplied options, or a score against ordered levels. [Jev from TypeSafe](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction) offers a similar decision-oriented interface, with typed questions and probability distributions. These are a different building block from asking a chat model to write an answer and then parsing it.

This could fit a lot of the repetitive judgment calls in VC infrastructure:

- Classify an inbound message as a pitch, portfolio update, LP request, or something else
- Route a company to the relevant sector team using its description and your coverage rules
- Flag whether a portfolio update contains a runway concern that needs review
- Rank retrieved passages for relevance before a larger model drafts a briefing
- Decide whether a request needs document search, a database query, or more extensive reasoning

The appeal is putting small judgments into a pipeline where a full reasoning loop would add latency and cost. Use ordinary code for exact rules such as dates, permissions, and numerical thresholds. Use a decision model where interpreting language is the missing step. Keep generation for outputs that need it, such as a memo or explanation.

Evaluate these models on labeled examples from the fund. Measure missed concerns as well as false alarms, and set thresholds for routing uncertain cases to review. A returned probability is an estimate to test on your data. It isn't a verified fact about the company. Keep the evidence with the result so the reviewer can inspect it.

## Smarter Harnesses: More Than a Better Model

A harness is the software around the model: the execution loop, tools, context management, saved state, and checks that determine what happens next. Improvements here can change whether an agent finishes the work, even with the same underlying model.

[Anthropic's harness engineering work](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents) describes agents making incremental progress across sessions using persistent artifacts and explicit checks. Coding tools also offer execution capabilities directly: [Claude Code subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents) can handle delegated tasks, while [Cursor cloud agents](https://prod.cursor.com/help/ai-features/cloud-agents) run work in remote environments. Parallel and background work are available capabilities, rather than a future you need to design around.

For a fund workflow, the useful harness improvements are concrete:

- Resume a document-processing run from its last completed item after a failure
- Load the relevant company records and source documents instead of carrying every prior result in context
- Validate extracted figures and retry the failed step, rather than rerunning the entire board-pack analysis
- Save intermediate work and pause for a reviewer before updating shared records
- Escalate an ambiguous case to a stronger model or a person

Decision APIs could provide some of those routing and checking steps. That's an architectural option to evaluate, not a reason to replace deterministic checks with model calls.

Before writing a custom harness, check what your agent product or SDK already handles. Own the fund-specific workflow and data connections; avoid maintaining a second implementation of session management just to run a weekly briefing.

## Parallel Development and Reusable Skills

Coding agents can edit, run, and check changes across a codebase. For a solo engineer maintaining several fund tools, the question is how much work you can delegate without becoming the bottleneck for review and integration.

**Start with one agent and a task you can check**

Use the same principles from [Choosing Your Stack](/guide/part-3-technical-foundations/choosing-your-stack): boring technology, clear code, and good documentation. Give the agent a concrete outcome, a bounded scope, and a way to verify the result. You still need to review what it changes.

**When orchestration helps**

[Cursor](https://cursor.com/docs/configuration/worktrees), [ChatGPT/Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/environments/git-worktrees), and [Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/common-workflows) support parallel work through agents and separate workspaces. Git worktrees give each session its own checkout and branch, keeping uncommitted edits separate.

Worktrees don't eliminate integration problems. An agent changing an API and another building its frontend still need an agreed interface. Separate branches don't automatically share changes. Someone needs to reconcile assumptions, review the combined result, and run the checks. That's part of the work, even if an agent helps with it.

Start with independent tasks: fixes in separate tools, a documentation update alongside a feature, or an independent review. Splitting a tightly coupled feature across five agents can create more coordination work than it saves.

**Are frameworks just token burners?**

They can be. Repeated planning, large shared contexts, retries, and agents reviewing each other all cost money and time. A framework earns its place when it makes a workflow easier to control, inspect, or recover after failure.

Before adopting one, compare it with a single agent doing the same kind of task:

- Did the result pass the same acceptance checks?
- How long did it take to reach a reviewed, usable result?
- What was the total model and execution cost, including retries?
- How much time did you spend correcting, reviewing, and integrating the work?

For a recurring fund workflow, test representative inputs, including messy ones. A pitch-deck extraction pipeline might only need fixed steps with validation and retries. Don't add a team of agents when a straightforward workflow does the job.

**Skills: Reuse the instructions that work**

Skills package repeatable instructions and supporting resources for an agent to use when needed. A useful skill at your fund might describe how to draft a valuation memo from approved inputs, follow your internal application's conventions, or check a data import.

[Superpowers](https://github.com/obra/superpowers) and [Matt Pocock's skills](https://github.com/mattpocock/skills) are examples of reusable development workflows.

You don't need to write the skill from scratch. Do the same task a few times with Claude or ChatGPT, correcting the output as you go. Once you're happy with the process, ask it to turn those sessions into a skill, including the examples, corrections, and preferences you've established. This captures how you actually do the work instead of making you specify the whole process upfront. Try it on the next instance of the task and refine anything it missed.

**Execution environments**

When an agent needs to execute code, services such as [Modal Sandboxes](https://modal.com/products/sandboxes), [Vercel Sandbox](https://vercel.com/docs/vercel-sandbox), and [Sprites](https://sprites.dev/) provide isolated execution environments. Compare persistence, network controls, credential handling, and how a run is stopped or recovered. The security chapter covers how to limit access around those environments.

## Files, Retrieval, and Structured Data

Start with the simplest way to give an agent the evidence it needs. For a small collection of investment memos, that may mean searching files and reading the relevant documents. You don't need a vector database just to try answering questions about your research.

**When working directly with files makes sense**

An agent with file tools can search filenames and text, read documents, and follow references. Keep documents in formats your team can maintain. Markdown works well for text research; PDFs and spreadsheets need suitable readers. You don't need to migrate everything into a git repository to make it useful to an agent.

This is a good starting point for bounded tasks: compare these five memos, summarize this board pack, or find our previous research on a company. Keep links to the source documents so someone can check the answer.

**When retrieval earns its place**

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) means finding relevant evidence and providing it to the model. Vector search is one option; keyword search and database queries can also supply that evidence. An agent searching files is still retrieving information.

Larger context windows don't make retrieval obsolete. You still need to find the right material, respect access permissions, handle updates, and control the cost of repeatedly reading documents. Compacted session summaries can lose details, so keep the underlying evidence available.

For a shared research assistant over years of memos, indexed search may be more useful than repeatedly scanning folders. Try keyword search first, then semantic or hybrid search if the questions need it. Check whether the system finds the right evidence before judging how convincing its answers sound.

[turbopuffer](https://turbopuffer.com/docs) supports vector and full-text search with metadata filtering, so you can combine semantic similarity with keyword matching in a RAG pipeline. Its storage architecture is built around object storage, with memory and SSD caching for queries.

**Use structured data for structured questions**

Ask your database which companies raised a Series A last quarter. Don't ask an agent to infer the list from a folder of documents when you already have funding-round records. Use SQL for filters, counts, and aggregations, as covered in [Data Warehousing](/guide/part-3-technical-foundations/data-warehousing).

A [knowledge graph](/guide/part-3-technical-foundations/knowledge-graphs) can help with relationship questions such as paths between founders, employers, and co-investors. Those relationships remain useful even when a model can read longer documents. Start with relationships in your relational database and add graph infrastructure when actual queries justify it.

**Test before adding infrastructure**

Collect questions your team actually asks and identify the evidence needed to answer them. Include questions where the right response is "we don't have enough information." Compare file search with indexed retrieval on answer accuracy, source coverage, permissions, latency, and cost.

These approaches can work together: query the CRM for companies, retrieve relevant memos, then read the original documents for detail.

## Model Choice and Evaluation

Don't bake one model ranking into your architecture. A decision model, a fast general model, and a more capable reasoning model can serve different steps of the same workflow.

Keep a small evaluation set for each recurring task: documents with checked extractions, questions with known source evidence, or examples of correct routing. Compare quality, latency, cost per completed task, and time spent correcting results. Run it again when you change the model, instructions, or tools.

Try better context and clearer task boundaries before fine-tuning. Fine-tuning becomes worth investigating when you have a recurring failure, enough labeled examples, and a way to measure improvement.

## Staying Current

**Look at what larger engineering teams are building**

Engineering teams outside VC have the resources to experiment with tooling you might eventually use yourself. Ramp's [Inspect](https://inspect.com/) is a good example: a collaborative cloud coding agent built for its own team. Their [engineering write-up](https://engineering.ramp.com/post/why-we-built-our-background-agent) explains how they gave it a working development environment, access to debugging tools, and the ability to verify changes with tests and browser previews.

These write-ups can be more useful than another model announcement. Look at how teams assemble context, make environments reproducible, and bring people into review. You can borrow individual pieces for your fund's setup without building the whole platform.

**General tech communities**

- **[X](https://x.com)**: Follow engineers building in the AI space, VC tech practitioners, and companies building tools for VCs. The signal-to-noise ratio is low, but you'll see emerging tools and approaches before they're widely adopted.

- **[Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com)**: The Show HN section surfaces new tools and libraries. The comments often contain practical wisdom from people who've tried things in production. Good for understanding what's actually working versus what's just hype.

**VC-specific resources**

- **[Data Driven VC](https://datadrivenvc.io/)**: Community and resources specifically for people building data infrastructure at VC funds. Much better signal than general tech communities for VC-specific challenges.

- **[VC Stack](https://www.vcstack.com/data-software-tools)**: Directory of data providers and software for VC funds. Useful for finding options before you decide to build.

- **[Vestberry VC Day](https://vcday.vestberry.com/)**: Conferences focused on VC operations and technology. Good for understanding what funds of all sizes are building and what tools are emerging in the ecosystem.

## The Bottom Line

Try the existing application layer before building another interface. Claude, ChatGPT, and specialist products increasingly supply workflows that used to require a custom tool.

Decision APIs are worth testing for classification, routing, and scoring inside those workflows. Smarter harnesses can handle the surrounding execution: context, state, retries, and review. Your fund's data, workflow definitions, and evaluation examples make these pieces useful together.
