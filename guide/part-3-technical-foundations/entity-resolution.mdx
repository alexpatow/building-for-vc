If a partner wants total funding in a market and one provider covers it, run the analysis there. For employee counts, a single [people-data source](/guide/part-3-technical-foundations/data-providers/people-data) may also cover the question. You need entity resolution when the answer depends on joining records from different places.

For example, you want funding rounds from PitchBook, hiring history from a people-data provider, and your team’s notes from the CRM. All three can name the same company differently. If you guess wrong, you can double-count its funding or miss that a partner already passed.

Getting this right was a big part of what made EQT’s Motherbrain work. Each provider assigns its own IDs. Before you can ask which founders worked together or which investors backed the same companies, you need to know when two of those IDs refer to the same company or person.

## The Entity Resolution Problem

**Company name variations**: You might see ‘Stripe, Inc.’, ‘Stripe Inc’, and ‘Stripe Payments’ across three sources. Punctuation is easy to normalize. Whether a payments subsidiary and its parent should share a record depends on whether you’re tracking a brand or the legal entity that signed a deal.

**Location variations**: "San Francisco, CA" vs "San Francisco, California" vs "SF" vs "San Francisco, CA, USA". Same location, different representations. Some sources use city and state, others add country, others use abbreviations.

**People name variations**: "Alex Smith" vs "Alexander Smith" vs "A. Smith". Same person, different formats. People also change names (marriage, personal preference), use nicknames professionally, and have names that transliterate differently from other languages.

**Data staleness**: A company moved offices, changed their URL, or was acquired. Some data sources have updated information, others don't. Which is correct? How do you handle temporal changes while maintaining entity consistency?

**Missing data**: Not every source has every field. Crunchbase might have a LinkedIn URL, but PitchBook doesn't. One source has founding date, another doesn't. You need to match entities even with incomplete information.

## Why Entity Resolution Matters for VC

A sourcing feed surfaces Acme Robotics just before the pipeline meeting. Under its former name, the CRM already has the partner’s pass notes and the milestone they asked the founders to come back with. Linking the records changes the conversation from “who’s going to reach out?” to “did they hit that milestone?”

The same problem shows up in analytics. Matching Acme across two funding feeds lets you bring its rounds together. You still need to check whether the two entries for its Series A describe one round before you add up the money raised. In a network graph, splitting Acme across two nodes can hide a warm introduction because the founder is attached to one node and your co-investor to the other.

## Start Simple: See How Far You Can Get

**Match on company websites.** Parse the URL and compare the hostnames. For example, `stripe.com`, `https://www.stripe.com/`, and a link to a page on the Stripe website can all point you to the same company. Lowercase the hostname and standardize a leading `www.`. Keep the original URL too. Two startups hosted on the same publishing platform can share a hostname, and subsidiaries sometimes share their parent’s website.

**Match on LinkedIn URLs.** Compare the profile itself. Stripping tracking parameters from `linkedin.com/company/stripe/?trk=feed` still leaves you with the company’s page. Stripping the whole path leaves you with LinkedIn. Keep company and personal profiles separate, and use the provider’s profile ID when it supplies one.

**Match people on email addresses.** A direct email address is a useful second signal when a LinkedIn URL is missing. A shared inbox such as `info@example.com` tells you very little about which founder you met. An old work email can also explain why two records for the same person disagree.

**Exact string matches**: After normalizing names (lowercase, remove punctuation, standardize "Inc" vs "Incorporated"), exact string matches catch many cases. "Stripe Inc" and "Stripe, Inc." become "stripe inc" and match.

Measure how many records those rules resolve and inspect a sample of the matches. The remaining cases tell you whether you need fuzzy matching, better source data, or someone who knows the companies.

**Why this matters**: Building an entity resolution system is expensive in engineering time and ongoing maintenance. Every hour spent on entity resolution is an hour not spent on research platforms, deal flow tools, or portfolio support. If simple rules handle most cases, that might be enough for your fund's scale.

## Entity Resolution as a Service

- **[Senzing](https://senzing.com/)**: Real-time entity resolution focused on messy people and organization data. Available on AWS Marketplace, deploys in your infrastructure for data privacy and compliance. Good for company matching with inconsistent names and addresses.

- **[Tamr](https://www.tamr.com/entity-resolution)**: AI-native entity resolution with specific B2B capabilities. Offers automated matching and supports Dun & Bradstreet enrichment for company data. Focuses on master data management and customer 360 use cases.

- **[AWS Entity Resolution](https://aws.amazon.com/entity-resolution/)**: Amazon's native service with flexible, configurable workflows. Integrates directly with other AWS services if you're already in that ecosystem.

These products differ in what they resolve, how you deploy them, and whether they match your records to an external dataset or to each other. Test with your own companies and people before choosing one. A generic customer-data demo won't tell you how well it handles startup rebrands or two founders with the same name.

**Compare the whole cost**: Enterprise contracts can be expensive, but don't assume every option requires one. [AWS Entity Resolution publishes usage-based pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/entity-resolution/pricing/). Include setup, repeated matching runs, enrichment, and ongoing review when comparing it with building your own rules.

**Use a library before writing a matching engine**: [Splink](https://moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink/index.html) is an open-source option for probabilistic record linkage. It gives you matching machinery, but you still need to prepare the data, evaluate matches, and run the pipeline. It isn't a finished application for your investment team.

**Keep your own canonical IDs**: Store vendor IDs as mappings to your internal entities. You can replace the matching engine without changing every application's company IDs. Check that you can export both mappings and review decisions before committing to a vendor.

Buy when a service performs well on your data and saves enough ongoing work to justify its cost. Use simple rules or a library when they cover your needs. Either way, budget for human review.

## Building Your Own Entity Resolution Service

**The canonical ID approach**: Assign one internal ID per entity. Every company or person gets exactly one canonical ID in your system. All records from external sources (Crunchbase, PitchBook, LinkedIn, your CRM) map to these canonical IDs. When you query for a company, you query by canonical ID, which pulls data from all sources that reference that entity.

**Matching strategies for companies**:

When comparing two company records to decide if they're the same entity, match on multiple fields:

- **Name**: Fuzzy string matching with normalization. Convert to lowercase, remove punctuation, standardize abbreviations ("Inc", "Corp", "Ltd"). Use string similarity algorithms (Levenshtein distance, Jaro-Winkler) to catch typos and minor variations.

- **Domain and LinkedIn page.** If a name change breaks the text match, a shared company page and a consistent domain history may still let you link the records. If those identifiers point to different companies, send the pair to review.

- **Location**: City and country provide supporting evidence. "San Francisco" and "SF" should normalize to the same value. Location alone isn't sufficient (multiple companies in the same city), but combined with name similarity it strengthens confidence.

- **Founding date**: If both records have founding dates and they're close (within a year), that supports a match. But founding dates are often missing or wrong in data sources, so don't rely on this.

- **Description similarity**: Company descriptions can be compared using embeddings or keyword analysis. If two companies with similar names also have similar descriptions, higher confidence they're the same entity.

**Probabilistic matching.** Score candidate pairs using several signals. Check the scores against matches and mismatches your team has verified, then set thresholds for automatic matching, review, and rejection.

For example, two records called Acme in San Francisco are worth comparing. If both identify the same founder and company page, you have much stronger evidence. If one sells logistics software and the other is a fintech with a different founding team, the name and city alone would give you a bad merge. Look at examples on either side of your threshold before you put it into production.

**Matching strategies for people**:

- **LinkedIn profile.** Compare the full personal profile or its ID across sources.
- **Email.** An exact match on an individual address can connect a CRM contact to a founder record.
- **Name and employer.** Use employment dates as well as names. An old record for Alex Smith at one company and a new record for Alexander Smith at another could be the same person after a move. Two people with the same name at a large employer could also be different people.

**The incremental matching workflow**:

When a new company record arrives from a data source:

1. Look up the source and its record ID. If you’ve already mapped that record, use its existing mapping unless new evidence calls it into question.
2. Look for candidates by normalized domain, LinkedIn profile, or name and location. Compare the other fields before linking a new source record.
3. Link high-confidence matches. Show ambiguous pairs to a reviewer, including the evidence for and against the match.
4. If there’s no plausible match, create a canonical entity and save the source mapping so the next import can find it.

### What gets stored after a match

Suppose a fund has three records for the same business. The CRM calls it Acme and has the partner’s notes. The funding provider calls it Acme Robotics Inc. and supplies two rounds. The people-data feed has an older name, Acme Systems. Once someone confirms the match, keep the three records and map them to one internal company ID. A CRM record ID and a funding-provider ID might both happen to be 42, so include the source when looking up an external ID.

| Source       | Source ID | Internal company ID | Evidence                                       |
| ------------ | --------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| CRM          | 42        | 701                 | Company record reviewed by the deal lead       |
| Funding feed | 42        | 701                 | Website and company profile agree              |
| People feed  | 319       | 701                 | Reviewer confirmed the former name and founder |

Now a partner can pull up Acme and see all three sources. You still have to decide which source to use for headcount and which reported the round. And if the people feed was actually describing another company, you can move that one mapping without detaching the original deal notes. If you’d overwritten everything with a single merged row, you’d have to reconstruct where each fact came from.

## Human-in-the-Loop: You'll Need It

Automated matching will never be 100% accurate. You will have false positives (incorrectly merged entities) and false negatives (incorrectly split entities). You need tooling for humans to review and correct mistakes.

**Review interface.** For CRM cleanup, start with the CRM’s own duplicate tools. For cross-source matches, show colleagues the two records, where each came from, and which fields matched. Let them confirm, reject, or defer a pair and keep their decision.

Record who made the decision and why. If an associate has already ruled out a pair, the next nightly import should remember that. If the provider later supplies a missing company page, the reviewer can see what changed.

**Merge interface**: When you discover two canonical entities that should be one (the algorithm missed a match), you need to merge them. This means:

- Choosing which canonical ID to keep
- Mapping all external references from the old ID to the new ID
- Combining data from both entities into a single record
- Preserving history (you want to know these were merged in case you need to undo it)

**Split interface**: When you discover one canonical entity that should be two (the algorithm incorrectly merged different companies), you need to split them:

- Create a new canonical ID for the second entity
- Decide which external references belong to which entity (this is hard - you're untangling merged data)
- Recalculate any analytics or relationships that were affected by the incorrect merge

## Common Pitfalls

**Matching too aggressively**: False positives are painful. If you incorrectly merge two different companies, all their data gets mixed together. Investors, employees, funding rounds, relationships: all incorrectly attributed to a single entity. This corrupts your analytics and is hard to untangle. Be conservative with automatic matching. When in doubt, flag for human review.

**Matching too conservatively**: False negatives are also painful. If you don't merge the same company across sources, you have duplicate entities, fragmented data, and wrong analytics. Finding the right balance between false positives and false negatives is the art of entity resolution.

Review some records your rules left unmatched as well as the pairs they joined. Looking only at accepted matches can make a system look excellent while it quietly leaves half the market map in duplicate. Choose the threshold for the job: a suggested duplicate in a CRM is cheap to dismiss, but an automatic merge that reassigns board materials is expensive to undo.

For a vendor trial, give each candidate the same export of known matches, known non-matches, and uncertain pairs. Include companies that share a name, companies that changed their domain, founders who moved, and records with a missing website. Keep the uncertain ones separate when you score the results. A product that sends the hard cases to an analyst may be doing exactly what you want.

Look at the review queue with the person who will own it. If the tool produces fifty suggested merges a week, can that person see the source records, explain a decision to the deal lead, and undo yesterday’s mistake? Include that work when you compare the managed service with running your own rules.

**Name changes over time.** Keep former names and domains with the dates they were in use. When an analyst looks up Acme Systems from an old memo, they should find the company now called Acme Robotics and still be able to tell which name appeared in the memo.

**Not handling acquisitions properly**: Is Instagram a separate entity from Meta? For some purposes yes (you want to track Instagram as a product), for others no (financially it's part of Meta). You might need different entity models for different use cases: legal entities (separate companies) vs. operational entities (products within a parent company).
